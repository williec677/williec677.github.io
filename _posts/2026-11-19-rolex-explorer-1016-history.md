Some vintage Rolex references are loud. The Explorer 1016 is not one of them. No date, no rotating bezel, no crown guards, just a 36mm steel case and a black dial with three numerals: 3, 6, and 9. And yet, ask a room full of vintage collectors which Rolex they would keep if they could only keep one, and the 1016 comes up again and again.

It is the reference that made the Explorer what it is today. Here is its story, from the first gilt dials of 1963 to the last matte examples of 1989.

## Where the 1016 came from

The Explorer arrived in 1953, born for high altitude Himalayan climbing, as Hodinkee puts it. Bob's Watches notes that the 1016 came along ten years later, and LeWatchBuyers says it replaced the reference 6610. LeWatchBuyers dates the run from 1963 to 1989, which makes it the longest running Explorer reference of all. You will see earlier start dates in some places. Hodinkee's guide, for example, shows 1016 gilt dials from as early as 1960, while LeWatchBuyers argues that the 1960 attribution comes from overlap with the outgoing 6610. Treat any claim of a pre 1963 1016 as something to verify with the case serial.

The recipe stayed remarkably consistent for 26 years:

* **Case:** 36mm Oyster case in stainless steel with a smooth bezel and no crown guards.
* **Crystal:** domed acrylic.
* **Water resistance:** 100m, which Bob's Watches and LeWatchBuyers both note was a step up for the Explorer line.
* **Lugs:** 20mm, according to both LeWatchBuyers and WatchCharts.
* **Dial:** black, with luminous 3, 6, and 9 numerals and a triangle at 12.

[Hodinkee's Explorer reference guide](https://www.hodinkee.com/articles/rolex-explorer-reference-points) points out that the 1016 introduced the "Superlative Chronometer Officially Certified" text to the Explorer dial, a line that still appears on Rolex dials today.

## The movements: 1560, then 1570

Two automatic movements powered the 1016, and this is where a lot of listings get it wrong.

* **Calibre 1560** came first. Hodinkee notes it ran at 18,000 vibrations per hour.
* **Calibre 1570** replaced it in the mid 1960s, per Hodinkee, and ran at a faster 19,800 vibrations per hour. LeWatchBuyers puts the change at around 1966.

Here is the detail that trips people up. Hacking, the feature that stops the seconds hand when you pull the crown to set the time, did not arrive with the 1570. Hodinkee places the addition of hacking in the early 1970s, and LeWatchBuyers narrows it to roughly 1971 or 1972. So a late 1960s 1016 with a non hacking 1570 can be completely correct. Do not let a seller, or a forum post, tell you otherwise.

One honest note on sources: Bob's Watches places the 1560 to 1570 change in the mid 1970s. Hodinkee and LeWatchBuyers both put it in the mid 1960s, so that is the date I use. When sources disagree, the movement serial and the case serial together are your best guide, and a good watchmaker can confirm what you have.

## Gilt dials: the early years

The first 1016s wore glossy black dials with gold colored printing. Collectors call these gilt dials. Hodinkee explains they were made with an expensive galvanic process that produced crisp, deep printing.

Gilt dials are the most collected and the most valuable 1016 dials. They are also the most faked and restored, because the gap in price between a gilt and a matte example is huge. Bob's Watches adds another wrinkle: Rolex service centers often replaced gilt dials with later matte dials during servicing. That means a 1960s 1016 with a matte dial is not automatically a fake. It may simply have had a service dial fitted decades ago.

### The exclamation dial

Some early 1016 dials carry a small luminous dot just below the 6 o'clock marker. Collectors call this the exclamation dial. LeWatchBuyers dates it to roughly 1963 to 1964 and links it to the period when Rolex was reducing radium content in its lume. It is a dating clue rather than a decoration, and it commands a premium.

## Matte dials: the long middle and the end

Hodinkee places the switch from gilt to matte around 1966 to 1967. Matte dials trade the glossy gold print for flat white printing on a textured black surface. They were simpler to produce, and they look even more purposeful.

Because matte production ran for more than 20 years, collectors split matte dials into families based on small changes in the coronet, the font, and the lume plots. LeWatchBuyers groups them as Mark I through Mark V, with the Mark I the most desirable. LeWatchBuyers says the earliest matte dials, from roughly 1967, carry a coronet collectors call the frog foot for the splayed look of its points. ## The end of the run

In 1989, Rolex replaced the 1016 with the reference 14270. LeWatchBuyers notes it brought a sapphire crystal and applied hour markers with white gold surrounds. That was the end of the acrylic, tritium, all printed Explorer.

Today's descendant is the [Explorer 36 reference 124270](https://www.rolex.com/en-us/watches/explorer/m124270-0001), which you see in the photo above. It keeps the 36mm size and the 3, 6, 9 dial, and it uses calibre 3230 with approximately 70 hours of power reserve. On rolex.com on November 13, 2026, it carried an MSRP of $7,900 USD. Rolex does not publish an official photo of a vintage 1016, which is why I used the modern model here.

## What a 1016 sells for in 2026

Here is the market picture as of mid November 2026.

* **WatchCharts:** an estimated private sale price of about **$15,624 USD** as of November 12, 2026, and about $18,062 USD to buy from a pre owned dealer ([WatchCharts, Explorer 1016](https://watchcharts.com/watch_model/28168-rolex-explorer-1016/overview)). That is a model estimate drawn from sales and listings, not a single sold price.
* **LeWatchBuyers** describes honest matte examples trading in the mid teens to low twenties as of mid 2026, with original gilt dials running about $25,000 to $45,000 USD or more ([LeWatchBuyers](https://www.lewatchbuyers.com/watch-education/vintage-rolex-1016-explorer/)).

The range is wide because originality drives everything. Two 1016s with the same wear can be thousands apart if one has a refinished dial or relumed hands. ## Five checks before you buy a 1016

1. **Date the case.** Find the serial between the lugs at 6 and compare it to the dial and movement. My guide to [reading Rolex serial and reference numbers](/how-to-read-rolex-serial-and-reference-numbers/) covers where to look.
2. **Match the dial to the era.** A gilt dial in a mid 1970s case or an early matte dial in a 1963 case means something was swapped. Service dials are honest parts, but they should be priced as service dials.
3. **Check the lume.** The hands and the dial plots should have aged together. Bright white hands on a cream dial are a replacement.
4. **Know your movement.** A non hacking 1570 in a late 1960s watch is correct. A hacking movement in an early gilt watch is a question.
5. **Look at the case.** Sharp lugs with visible bevels are worth paying for. Heavy polishing softens the case and lowers value.

## Why the 1016 still matters

The 1016 is the purest expression of the Rolex tool watch. It is small by today's standards, it reads instantly, and it has a 26 year production run that gives you a huge range of dials and price points to choose from. If you want one vintage Rolex that goes with absolutely everything, this is it.

## Quick takeaways

* The Explorer 1016 ran from 1963 to 1989: 36mm, acrylic crystal, 100m, 20mm lugs.
* Calibre 1560 first, then calibre 1570 from the mid 1960s. Hacking arrived only around 1971 or 1972.
* Gilt dials until about 1966 to 1967, then matte dials. Service dials are common.
* WatchCharts estimated a private sale price of about $15,624 USD on November 12, 2026.
* The 14270 replaced it in 1989. Today's Explorer 36 124270 has an MSRP of $7,900 USD.
