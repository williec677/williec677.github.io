The vintage Tudor Submariner gets less attention than its Rolex cousin, and that is part of the appeal. The Snowflake versions in particular have a look no other diver of the era had: square markers and a square tipped hour hand that stays legible in poor light. Tudor still uses that hand today. If you are thinking about one, here is what the references mean, why the French Navy matters, and the two problems that can cost you the most money.

## Where the Snowflake came from

Tudor introduced the 7016 around 1969 as the successor to the 7928, and it was the Submariner where Tudor switched to an ETA movement, the calibre 2483 ([Tudor Collector](https://tudorcollector.com/collection/7016-2/)). The date version is the 7021. The very first 7016s did not have Snowflake hands at all. They used a dial layout carried over from the 7928, in a case with semi pointed crown guards. The Snowflake layout followed soon after.

That layout was not a styling exercise. Tudor Collector explains that it came out of work with the Marine Nationale, the French Navy, whose divers had been using the 7928 and asked for a more legible dial and handset. Tudor's own history pages tell the same story ([Tudor](https://www.tudorwatch.com/en/inside-tudor/history/tudor-history-submariners-1969-to-1999)). The 7016 was also the first Tudor Submariner offered with a choice of blue or black dial and bezel.

## The references at a glance

| Reference | Introduced | Date | Movement |
|---|---|---|---|
| 7016 | around 1969 | No | ETA 2483 |
| 7021 | around 1969 | Yes | ETA family, with date |
| 9401 | around 1975 | No | ETA 2776, stop seconds |
| 9411 | around 1975 | Yes | ETA 2784, stop seconds |

Tudor Collector puts the end of the 7016 and 7021 around 1975, when the 9401 and 9411 replaced them, with serials roughly in the 620,000 to 780,000 range for 1968 to 1975. Some military 7016s turn up with serials in the 820,000s, which Tudor Collector attributes to cases set aside for special orders. Production years shift slightly by source, so treat them as approximate.

Later Tudor Submariners moved to Mercedes hands and triangle markers in the early 1980s, so a Snowflake is a fairly short window in a long line.

## The Marine Nationale connection

Tudor's relationship with the French Navy started in 1956, when the first watches went for testing to the Navy's underwater research group. Tudor became an official supplier in 1961, and the partnership ran into the early 1980s before Tudor revived it in 2021 ([Daniel Bourn](https://danielbourn.com/the-tudor-mn-military-submariner/), [Tudor](https://www.tudorwatch.com/en/inside-tudor/history/tudor-and-marine-nationale)).

Here is the detail most buyers miss. According to Daniel Bourn, Tudor MN Submariners looked exactly like civilian watches until 1974. The 7016 delivered that year was the first reference with the familiar MN case back engraving, marked MN74, and those watches generally sit in the 827,000 serial range. From 1975 the Navy received the 9401 with blue dial and matching blue bezel, and in the early 1980s 9401s with Mercedes hands and triangle markers.

### What counts as proof

An engraving is easy to add. Collectors look for a serial that matches Navy records or decommission paperwork, plus a configuration that fits the year. Be wary of a watch sold as MN on the strength of the case back alone. Ask what documentation exists and who has checked it. If the answer is vague, price the watch as a civilian Snowflake, because that is all you can prove.

## Problem one: dial rot

Early 7016 and 7021 Snowflake dials have a known problem. Tudor Collector writes that the paint and lacquer on these dials decayed at a rapid rate, often without any water getting in. The decay usually appears as bubbles under the surface, starting around the center of the dial. It affects blue and black dials alike, which also corrects an old myth that Tudor switched to blue because the black dials were worse ([Tudor Collector](https://tudorcollector.com/collection/7016-2/)).

The tritium plots on a bubble dial are often still in good shape, because it is the lacquer that breaks down. Light bubbling is accepted by many collectors as part of the story. Heavy bubbling makes the dial hard to read and lowers value. What you want to see is decay consistent with the rest of the watch, not a suspiciously fresh dial on a heavily worn case.

## Problem two: the service trap

This is the one that catches owners out. Timekeeper PH explains that a Rolex service center tends to return a watch to "as purchased" condition, which can include a new dial, hands, and bezel, and that replaced parts are generally not returned ([Timekeeper PH](https://www.timekeeper.ph/movement-clinic/snowflake-dial-rot-and-the-service-trap)). A degraded tritium dial is exactly the kind of part that gets replaced. On a Snowflake, the original dial is a large share of the value, so a factory service can cost you far more than the invoice.

The same article notes that Rolex's spare parts commitment runs for at least 35 years after a model is discontinued, which puts 1970s Tudors well outside it. In practice, that means an independent watchmaker who knows vintage Tudor and agrees in writing to keep every original part.

## How to check one

1. **Check the dial and hands together.** Original lume on the square hour hand should age with the dial plots.
2. **Read the serial between the lugs** and make sure the configuration fits the period.
3. **Look at the case back.** Civilian backs are normal. Navy engravings need paperwork.
4. **Ask about service history** and whether any part was replaced, and by whom.
5. **Check the movement** matches the reference, ETA 2483 family for the 7016 and 7021, and 2776 or 2784 for the 9401 and 9411.

## Modern alternatives

Tudor brought the Snowflake hand back in 2012 with the Pelagos, and it appears on several current models ([Tudor](https://www.tudorwatch.com/en/inside-tudor/history/tudor-history-submariners-1969-to-1999)). If you love the look but want a modern warranty and water resistance, a current Pelagos is the obvious place to start. If you want the vintage original, plan on patience. Good examples do not come up every week.

## Quick takeaways

* The 7016 and 7021 arrived around 1969. Snowflake hands came from French Navy requests for legibility.
* The 9401 and 9411 replaced them around 1975 with ETA 2776 and 2784.
* MN watches looked civilian until 1974. Engraving alone is not proof.
* Early Snowflake dials can bubble, black and blue alike.
* A factory service may replace the dial and keep the original. Choose your watchmaker carefully.
