My collection leans Rolex, but the watch I recommend most to friends starting out in vintage is a Seiko 6105. It has real history, a design that still looks modern, and a movement that keeps going. It is also one of the most frankenstein prone watches you can buy, because aftermarket parts are everywhere.

## Where the 6105 came from

Seiko launched the 6105 in 1968 as the successor to its first diver, the 62MAS. It was produced until about 1977 ([The Seiko Guy](https://www.theseikoguy.com/movements/6105), [Seiko diver timeline](https://www.theseikoguy.com/seiko-diver-timeline)). Two main case families exist.

## 6105-8000 and 6105-8009: the first generation

Introduced in 1968, the first generation came as the 6105-8000 for the Japanese market and the 6105-8009 for export. Key features:

* A roughly 41mm cushion case with the crown at 4 o'clock.
* 19mm lugs, which narrows strap choices.
* 150 meters of water resistance.
* A Hardlex mineral crystal.

Japanese market dials and casebacks differ from export pieces in small ways. Learn the differences before you pay a premium for one.

## 6105-8110 and 6105-8119: Captain Willard

The second generation arrived in 1970. The case grew to about 44mm, became asymmetric, and gained a protective shoulder around the crown. The crown locks with a push and twist bayonet action rather than a conventional screw down thread ([Mechanical Debt](https://mechanicaldebt.com/seiko-6105-willard)). This is the shape people mean when they say "Captain Willard."

The nickname comes from Apocalypse Now (1979). Martin Sheen's character, Captain Benjamin Willard, wears a 6105 in the film. There was no product placement deal. The 6105 was simply a watch that many soldiers bought at base exchanges during the Vietnam era, and it ended up on screen ([Mechanical Debt](https://mechanicaldebt.com/seiko-6105-willard)).

## 6105A vs 6105B

Calibre 6105 is a 17 jewel automatic running at 21,600 vibrations per hour, with a quickset date. It cannot be hand wound, so you start it with a gentle swing. Most 6105-8000 watches use the 6105A, which does not hack. Some of the last 8000s and the 8110, from July 1970, use the 6105B, which hacks, meaning the seconds hand stops when you pull the crown ([The Seiko Guy](https://www.theseikoguy.com/movements/6105)).

The B is nicer to set precisely. The A is not a problem. Neither is a deal breaker, but the movement should match the case era, and the seller should be clear about which one is inside.

## Reading a Seiko serial

Seiko serials on this era encode the production date. The first digit is the last digit of the year. The second digit is the month, with 1 to 9 for January to September, then 0 (which can look like an O when worn), N, and D for October, November, and December. So a serial starting "2N" on a 6105 would point to November 1972, because the 6105 was not in production in 1962 or 1982 ([Vintage Watch Studio](https://www.vintagewatchstudio.com/blogs/seiko/seiko-serial-numbers-explained)).

Check the movement date too. It should land near the case date. A movement years away from the case points to a swap.

## The franken problem

Because Seiko made so many divers with shared parts, and because aftermarket parts are cheap and good, a lot of 6105s are assembled from mixed sources. Things to check:

1. **Dial.** Aftermarket and redone dials are common. Check the printing quality, lume texture, and whether the lume ages in step with the hands.
2. **Hands.** Replacement hands are often a little off in shape or length.
3. **Bezel insert.** Aftermarket inserts are everywhere. Original inserts have specific fonts and colors that reference guides cover in detail.
4. **Crystal.** Many have been replaced with sapphire or a domed aftermarket crystal. That can be a fine wearing upgrade. It is not original.
5. **Case.** Look for heavy polishing, especially on the 8110's crown guard, and for pitting.

Most of these changes do not ruin the watch. They change its value. A 6105 with a replaced crystal and bezel insert is still a great watch to wear. It is just not a collector example, and it should not be priced like one.

## Modern alternatives

If the vintage hunt is not for you, Seiko has re released the design. The limited SLA033 came first, with the 8L35 movement, followed by the SPB151 and SPB153 in 2020 as more accessible re interpretations with the 6R35 movement, a 42.7mm case, and 200 meters of water resistance ([Hodinkee](https://www.hodinkee.com/articles/seiko-spb151-and-spb153-captain-willard-prospex-introducing)). They give you the shape with modern water resistance and a warranty.

## Practical ownership notes

* Service the movement if there is no recent record. Seals on a 50 year old diver are not to be trusted.
* Do not swim with it until the case has been pressure tested.
* For the 19mm first generation, a {% include aff.html network="amazon" url="https://www.amazon.com/s?k=19mm+rubber+dive+strap" text="19mm rubber strap" %} or a {% include aff.html network="amazon" url="https://www.amazon.com/s?k=19mm+nylon+watch+strap" text="nylon strap" %} looks right and saves the case from bracelet wear.

## Service realities

The 6105 movement is simple and well documented, and many parts interchange with its 6106 base calibre, according to The Seiko Guy. That is good news for owners. The less good news is that the bayonet crown and its gasket on the 8110 wear over time, and original case parts are getting harder to find. Ask a watchmaker who knows vintage Seiko before you buy if you are not sure, and budget for a service the moment the watch arrives.

## Quick takeaways

* 6105-8000/8009: 1968, 41mm cushion case. 6105-8110/8119: 1970, 44mm asymmetric case with crown guard.
* Captain Willard comes from Apocalypse Now, and it was not a paid placement.
* 6105A does not hack. 6105B does. Neither hand winds.
* Read the serial: first digit year, second digit month, with 0, N, D for the last three months.
* Expect aftermarket parts. Price originality, not just looks.
