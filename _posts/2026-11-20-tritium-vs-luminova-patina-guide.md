Patina sells watches. A creamy, even tone on the hour markers can make a common reference feel special, and buyers pay for it. That is exactly why lume is one of the most manipulated parts of a vintage watch. Here is how I read it.

## The four eras of lume

Watchmakers have used four main families of luminous material, and each leaves a signature on the dial.

**Radium** was used until around the early 1960s, with Bob's Watches putting the Rolex switch at 1963. It glows on its own because it is radioactive, and its half life is about 1,600 years. The paint binder breaks down long before the radium does, so old radium dials often look burnt or crumbly. Radium dials are a safety issue as well as a collecting one. Do not open or scrape them.

**Tritium** replaced radium from the early 1960s until the late 1990s. It also glows without charging, but its half life is about 12.3 years ([Wikipedia, tritium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tritium)). A dial from 1985 has gone through several half lives, which is why old tritium barely glows today. Rolex marked these dials with "T SWISS T" or "SWISS T<25" at 6 o'clock, signalling tritium under the regulatory limit.

**Luminova** arrived in the late 1990s. It is a photoluminescent material, meaning it stores light and releases it. Rolex dials from around 1998 to 1999 often read only "SWISS" at 6.

**Super LumiNova** followed around 2000, and Rolex dials began reading "SWISS MADE." Rolex later introduced its own Chromalight, which glows blue, starting with the Deepsea in 2008 ([Bob's Watches](https://www.bobswatches.com/rolex-blog/rolex-info/evolution-rolex-luminous.html)).

| Text at 6 (Rolex) | Lume | Approximate era |
|---|---|---|
| SWISS (no T) on older dials | Radium | Before early 1960s |
| T SWISS T / SWISS T<25 | Tritium | Early 1960s to late 1990s |
| SWISS | Luminova | About 1998 to 1999 |
| SWISS MADE | Super LumiNova, later Chromalight | About 2000 onward |

The dating is approximate and varies by model. It is a cross check against the serial, not a stand alone answer.

## How patina actually forms

Tritium paint is a compound of tritium and a phosphor in a binder. Over decades, the phosphor degrades and the binder yellows. Light, heat, humidity, and the exact paint batch all affect how fast and in which direction it ages. That is why two watches from the same year can look completely different.

Real aged tritium usually shows:

* **Consistency across the dial.** All plots age together, although slight variation is normal.
* **Matching hands.** Hands aged in the same conditions should be close in tone.
* **Texture.** Under a loupe, old lume has a slightly granular, dry look, sometimes with small cracks.

## Relume and fake patina

A relume is fresh luminous paint applied over or in place of the original. Some relumes are honest repairs. Many are done to fake an aged look, using tinted modern paint.

Signs I look for:

1. **Lume that is too thick or too neat,** with crisp edges and a glossy surface.
2. **Hands and dial that match perfectly in a way that looks painted,** especially when the case shows heavy wear.
3. **Lume overflow** onto the dial surface around the plots.
4. **Era mismatch.** A "T SWISS T" dial with plots that glow like a new watch.

## The glow test

This is the most useful ten second check I know. Charge the dial with a UV flashlight or bright light for a few seconds, then turn off the lights.

* **Original old tritium** gives a weak, short glow or almost nothing, because the tritium is largely spent. Toronto Watch Exchange describes a 1970s or 1980s dial as flashing briefly under UV and going dark almost at once ([Toronto Watch Exchange](https://torontowatchexchange.com/identify-rolex-service-dial-versus-original-dial/)).
* **Luminova and Super LumiNova** glow brightly and fade slowly.
* **A relumed tritium era dial** usually glows strongly, which gives it away.

The glow test is not perfect. Some modern paints are formulated to glow weakly, and sellers know about this check. Use it alongside the dial text, the serial, and the hands.

## Service dials and hands

When older Rolex watches went in for service, Rolex often replaced tritium dials and hands with Luminova versions. These are genuine Rolex parts, but they change the watch's collector value. Look for "SWISS" or "SWISS MADE" text on a case with an older serial. Rolex service dials usually have bright white plots that glow strongly. Sometimes the owner kept the original dial, which is worth asking about.

## A note on safety

Tritium watches are generally considered low risk to wear, because tritium's weak radiation does not get through the crystal and case. Radium is different. Old radium dials can still emit radiation and shed flakes of paint, so never open a radium era watch at home, never scrape or sand the dial, and let a watchmaker who handles radium do any work. If you collect pre 1960s pieces, store them sensibly and wash your hands after handling loose dials.

## Photographing lume honestly

If you sell or trade, show buyers the truth. Photograph the dial in daylight without filters, then a second shot in darkness right after a short charge. Mention whether the hands and dial are original. Honest lume photos build trust and save you from returns.

## Buying patina without overpaying

Pay for patina only when the rest of the story agrees. That means a dial era that fits the serial, hands that match, a glow that fits the material, and a seller who can show you the watch under different light. If any piece is off, value the watch as if the lume were ordinary.

## Quick takeaways

* Radium, tritium, Luminova, Super LumiNova, Chromalight: each era leaves text clues at 6.
* Tritium's 12.3 year half life means original vintage lume barely glows now.
* Bright glow on a T SWISS T dial suggests relume.
* Dial and hands should age together.
* Pay for patina only when the serial, dial text, hands, and glow tell one story.
