The most expensive mistake in vintage watches is not buying a fake. It is paying an asking price because it looked normal next to other asking prices. Listings anchor each other. A dealer prices against the dealer down the street, who priced against a listing that has been sitting for six months.

What you want is the price where a buyer and seller actually agreed. Here is how I find it.

## Asking price vs sold price

An asking price tells you what a seller hopes to get. A sold price tells you what the market paid on a specific date, for a specific condition. Those numbers can be far apart, especially on slow moving references or when the market is cooling.

So I treat every source in one of two buckets:

* **Supply sources** tell you what is available and what sellers want. Chrono24 is the biggest.
* **Transaction sources** tell you what traded. eBay sold listings and auction results are the most accessible.

## eBay sold listings: the free transaction database

eBay lets anyone filter search results to completed sales. On the website, search for the reference, then open the filters and tick **Sold items**. On the app, it is under Filter as well. Shortcut: add `LH_Sold=1&LH_Complete=1` to a search URL, like this {% include aff.html network="ebay" url="https://www.ebay.com/sch/i.html?_nkw=omega+speedmaster+145.022&LH_Sold=1&LH_Complete=1" text="sold search for the Speedmaster 145.022" %}.

Know the limits:

* **The window is short.** eBay's public sold search reaches back about 90 days ([Reclaim Stuff](https://www.reclaimstuff.com/ebay-sold-prices)). That is great for current comps, useless for long trends.
* **Best Offer hides the real number.** When a listing ends with an accepted offer, eBay shows the original asking price struck through, not the price that was paid ([SoldComps](https://www.soldcomps.io/blog/ebay-best-offer-accepted-price)). Treat those as "sold below this number," not as a comp.
* **Condition varies wildly.** Read every listing. A "sold" price for a watch with a service dial, polished case, and no papers says nothing about a full set example.
* **Some sales fall through.** Unpaid items exist. Look for clusters of similar prices rather than trusting one result.

My routine: pull the last 90 days, delete anything that is clearly a different configuration, ignore best offer strikethroughs, and look at the middle of what is left.

## Auction results: high quality data with a catch

Major auction houses publish results for past sales. Their catalogs are useful because they describe condition, originality, and provenance in detail, and specialists have examined the watch.

The catch is fees. The hammer price is not what the buyer paid. Auction houses add a buyer's premium on top, and the published "price realized" often already includes it. Read the house's terms for that sale to know which number you are seeing. Fratello's Speedmaster buying guide puts the premium at 20 to 25 percent on top of the hammer price ([Fratello](https://www.fratellowatches.com/buying-guide-omega-speedmaster-professional-145-022/)), and [Christie's explains its own tiered fees](https://www.christies.com/en/help/auction-help-library/understanding-auction-fees) in its help library. Rates vary by house and by price tier.

Two more quirks:

* **Auction watches skew to the top.** The best examples go to major houses, so results can overstate what an average example is worth.
* **Single results are noisy.** Two bidders who both want a watch can set a record that nobody repeats.

## Chrono24: use it for supply, not value

Chrono24 is the largest watch marketplace, and it is excellent for three things: seeing how many examples are for sale, comparing configurations side by side, and finding dealers who specialize in a reference. It shows asking prices. A listing that has sat for months at a high price is not evidence of value. It is evidence that nobody paid that.

I check {% include aff.html network="chrono24" url="https://www.chrono24.com/" text="Chrono24 listings" %} to understand supply and then go to sold data to understand price.

## Price data sites

Sites such as WatchCharts and EveryWatch aggregate listings and, in some cases, transaction data into price charts. Fratello's Speedmaster guide points to WatchCharts as a source of transaction data. These sites are useful for direction and speed: is a reference trending up or down this year? Read each site's methodology to see whether a chart is built from asking prices, sales, or a blend. A chart built from asking prices inherits the same anchoring problem.

## Building a comp you can defend

When I am serious about a watch, I write down five to ten comparable sales and include for each:

1. Date sold.
2. Where it sold.
3. Price paid, with fees noted.
4. Dial and bezel configuration.
5. Condition notes: polishing, service parts, lume.
6. Box and papers or not.

Then I adjust toward the watch I am looking at. Full set with original dial? Toward the top. Service dial, no papers? Toward the bottom. This is slower than glancing at a listing, and it is the reason I almost never overpay.

If you trade often, a simple {% include aff.html network="amazon" url="https://www.amazon.com/s?k=collector+inventory+log+book" text="inventory log book" %} or a spreadsheet keeps your comps and your purchase history in one place.

## Seller tools that show more history

If you sell on eBay, Seller Hub includes a Product Research tool, formerly Terapeak, that goes back much further than the public sold search and can show accepted best offer prices ([Reclaim Stuff](https://www.reclaimstuff.com/ebay-sold-prices)). It is free for sellers with an account. For anyone serious about trading, it is worth setting up.

## Why this blog does not quote prices casually

You will notice that the guides here rarely give a number. That is on purpose. A price without a date and a source goes stale within weeks and gets copied around the internet as fact. When I do quote one, it will be a range with the source and the date. Otherwise I would rather give you the method.

## Quick takeaways

* Asking prices anchor each other. Sold prices tell you the market.
* eBay sold filter covers about 90 days. Ignore best offer strikethroughs.
* Auction results need the buyer's premium accounted for and skew toward top examples.
* Use Chrono24 for supply and configuration, not for value.
* Build a dated comp list matched to condition, then adjust.
