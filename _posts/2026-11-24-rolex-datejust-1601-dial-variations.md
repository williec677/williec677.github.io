I wear a 2024 Datejust, and it taught me how good the Oyster case is on the wrist. The vintage 1601 is where that formula grew up. It is also one of the friendliest ways into vintage Rolex, because Rolex made a lot of them and the dial choices are endless.

That second part is both the joy and the risk.

## The 1601 in brief

The 1601 was produced from about 1959 to 1977, when the 16014 replaced it with a quickset date ([Tempus Europa](https://tempuseuropa.com/en/reference/rolex-datejust-1601)). Its siblings share the same case and movement and differ by bezel:

* **1600:** smooth steel bezel.
* **1601:** fluted bezel in white gold on a steel case (also produced in Rolesor yellow versions).
* **1603:** engine turned bezel.

Core specs: a 36mm case, 20mm lugs, an acrylic crystal with a cyclops, and 50 meters of water resistance. There is no quickset. To change the date you move the hands through midnight, which is a small ritual I happen to enjoy.

## Movements: 1565 vs 1575

Early 1601s use calibre 1565, beating at 18,000 vibrations per hour. Around 1965 Rolex moved to calibre 1575, which beats at 19,800. Hacking seconds came to the 1575 around 1971 to 1972 ([Tempus Europa](https://tempuseuropa.com/en/reference/rolex-datejust-1601)). As with the Submariner 1680, you will often see "1570" engraved on the 1575's bridge. That is correct.

Both calibres are robust and well understood. A recent service matters more than which one you get. If you plan to wear it daily, the later hacking 1575 is easier to set precisely.

## The dials collectors care about

### Pie pan

Many 1601 dials slope down toward the edge like the bottom of a pie dish. Light runs across the slope, which gives the dial depth. Pie pan is a shape, not a color. You will see it in silver, black, blue, champagne, and more.

### Text at 6 and lume

The fine print at 6 o'clock helps date the dial. Early radium dials carry "SWISS" alone up to about 1963. Then tritium dials read "SWISS T<25" and later "T SWISS T." A mismatch between the case serial and the dial text is the first hint that a dial was changed.

### Sigma

Sigma dials carry a small Greek σ on either side of "SWISS" at 6. They indicated solid gold hour markers and hands ([Chrono24 Magazine](https://www.chrono24.com/magazine/the-most-extraordinary-rolex-datejust-dials-p_50387/)). Not every 1970s dial has them. When present, they are a nice period detail.

### Linen

Linen dials have a fine crosshatched texture, like fabric. They are scarce on the 1601 and command interest. They are also easy to describe loosely, so look closely with a loupe for an actual woven pattern.

### Buckley

Buckley dials use printed Roman numerals in place of applied markers. They divide opinion. I like them on a smooth or fluted bezel. Some people hate them. Either way, they are distinctive.

### Wide boy

Wide boy dials use thicker baton markers. They are a subtle variant with a following of their own.

### Retailer co signed dials

You will see dials co signed with retailer names, Tiffany being the famous one. Genuine examples exist and can carry a large premium. Fakes and added signatures exist in far greater numbers. Treat a co signed dial as a separate, expert level purchase. Provenance and a specialist's opinion are non negotiable.

## Redials and refinished dials

A redial is a dial that has been stripped and reprinted. They were common decades ago as a cheap way to freshen old watches, and many are still in circulation. They are worth a fraction of an original.

Tells I check with a {% include aff.html network="amazon" url="https://www.amazon.com/s?k=10x+jewelers+loupe+triplet" text="10x loupe" %}:

1. **Printing quality.** Rolex printing is crisp, raised slightly, and evenly spaced. Blurry, thin, or uneven letters are a red flag.
2. **Font errors.** Compare the coronet, the Rolex text, and "Datejust" to verified originals.
3. **Lume and marker alignment.** Marker plots should sit centered and consistent.
4. **Wrong text for the era.** A tritium era serial with radium style text, or vice versa.
5. **Too perfect.** A spotless dial on a heavily worn case.

## Case and bracelet checks

The 1601 case is often polished. Look at the lugs: crisp edges and a visible bevel suggest light polishing at most. Soft, rounded lugs mean heavy polishing.

Jubilee bracelets from this era stretch. Some stretch is normal and fine. Heavy stretch makes the bracelet floppy and lowers value. Replacing it is possible, though period correct bracelets cost real money. In the meantime, a {% include aff.html network="amazon" url="https://www.amazon.com/s?k=20mm+leather+watch+strap" text="20mm leather strap" %} is a cheap way to wear the watch while you hunt. The 36mm Datejust has stayed at 20mm for generations, so the leather and suede picks on my [Datejust 36 strap page](/strap-finder/rolex-datejust-36/) suit a 1601 too.

## Buying a 1601 for daily wear

If you want a vintage Datejust to wear every day rather than a collector piece, the priorities shift. I would rank them like this:

1. **A recent service from a watchmaker you trust.** A 50 year old movement that has not been serviced will cost you more than the difference between two dials.
2. **A healthy case.** Thick lugs and a crisp bezel make the watch look right from across a room.
3. **A dial you love.** An honest silver pie pan with even aging beats a rare variant with questions around it.
4. **A comfortable bracelet.** A newer Jubilee or Oyster is fine for wearing, and you can always hunt for a period bracelet later.

The 1601 is not fragile, but it is not a modern Datejust either. Keep it away from water beyond hand washing unless the case has been pressure tested, and wind it a few turns if it has been sitting.

## Quick takeaways

* 1600 smooth, 1601 fluted, 1603 engine turned. Same 36mm case.
* Cal 1565 early, cal 1575 from about 1965, hacking from about 1972.
* Pie pan is a shape. Sigma marks solid gold markers and hands.
* Co signed dials are an expert purchase. Assume fake until proven.
* Check dial printing, era text, lug polish, and bracelet stretch every time.
